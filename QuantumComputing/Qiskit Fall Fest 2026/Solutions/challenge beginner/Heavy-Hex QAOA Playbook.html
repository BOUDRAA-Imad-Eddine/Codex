Heavy-Hex QAOA Playbook

The challenge rewards two things above all: how many qubits your circuit uses and how good your cut is. This page explains the problem, proposes a concrete solution, and shows what that solution measured on graphs from 12 to 156 qubits.

The problem in plain terms

Max-Cut. Colour every node of a graph black or white so that as many edges as possible join a black node to a white one. Each such edge is “cut”. Finding the best colouring is NP-hard for general graphs.

QAOA. Give each node a qubit. Start every qubit in an equal superposition, then repeat two steps p times: one two-qubit RZZ(γ) gate per edge, then one RX(2β) gate per qubit. A classical optimiser tunes γ and β so that the measured bitstrings cut many edges. In this notebook p = 1, so there is one γ and one β to find.

Why heavy-hex. IBM Heron and Eagle chips connect qubits in a heavy-hexagonal lattice: hexagons whose every side carries an extra qubit, so no qubit touches more than three others. If your problem graph is a piece of that lattice, every RZZ lands on two qubits that are already wired together. No SWAP gates are needed, and the circuit stays shallow.

The key observation

A heavy-hex graph has no odd cycles, so it is bipartite: a black-and-white colouring exists that cuts every single edge. The optimal cut therefore equals the number of edges, and a breadth-first search finds it in microseconds. I confirmed this on every subgraph used below.

What this means for the challenge. The quantum circuit is not solving a hard problem here. The score tests how well your circuit and your reported ratio α behave at scale. Read the grader’s rules for how obtainedResult is computed and whether classical post-processing is allowed. A one-pass local clean-up of sampled bitstrings would push α to 1.0 on any bipartite graph.

The proposed solution

Four steps. Steps 1 to 3 are measured on this page; step 4 needs Qiskit and hardware access.

Step 1

Take a real piece of the chip

Cut a connected, degree-≤3 subgraph out of the actual coupling map (for example a Heron fake backend), so zero SWAPs holds for the real device and not only by construction.

Step 2

Find γ and β analytically

At p = 1 each edge only “sees” the qubits within one step of its endpoints, at most 6. Evaluate each edge on that tiny neighbourhood, sum them, and scan a grid. This works for 156 qubits on a laptop.

Step 3

Schedule the cost layer by edge colour

Group edges so that no two in a group share a qubit. All RZZ gates in a group run together. Degree 3 needs 3 or 4 groups.

Step 4

Run with error mitigation

On a Heron backend or its noisy fake, use dynamical decoupling, Pauli twirling and readout mitigation, choose the best-calibrated region, and report the ratio you actually measured.

Reading the numbers

  • The average cut sits near 0.73 to 0.75 of the optimum at every size. That number barely moves as the graph grows, which is why γ and β found on a small instance transfer to the large one: γ drifts only from 0.80 to 0.72 and β stays at 1.178 (3π/8).
  • Averages are not the same as the best sampled cut. At 12 and 20 qubits the best of 4000 shots was the true optimum, but that gets harder as the graph grows. I could not sample the 64 to 156 qubit cases here, so no best-of-shots figure is claimed for them.
  • Greedy colouring needed 4 colours at 127 and 156 qubits. A bipartite graph of maximum degree 3 always admits 3 (König’s edge-colouring theorem), so a proper algorithm would save one layer.

The core code

This is the light-cone evaluator that produced the numbers above. It needs only numpy and networkx.

def edge_zz(G, e, gamma, beta):
    """<Z_u Z_v> at p=1, using only qubits within one step of the edge."""
    u, v = e
    nodes = sorted({u, v} | set(G[u]) | set(G[v]))
    loc = {x: i for i, x in enumerate(nodes)}
    n = len(nodes)
    edges = [(loc[a], loc[b]) for a, b in G.edges if a in (u, v) or b in (u, v)]
    psi = qaoa_state(n, edges, gamma, beta)       # exp(-i*gamma*H_C), then RX(2*beta)
    p = abs(psi) ** 2
    idx = np.arange(2 ** n)
    zu = 1 - 2 * ((idx >> (n - 1 - loc[u])) & 1)
    zv = 1 - 2 * ((idx >> (n - 1 - loc[v])) & 1)
    return float((p * zu * zv).sum())

def expected_cut(G, gamma, beta):
    return sum((1 - edge_zz(G, e, gamma, beta)) / 2 for e in G.edges)

This Qiskit sketch builds the edge-coloured circuit for the hardware step. I could not install Qiskit in the sandbox, so it has not been run. Treat it as a starting point and test it in your notebook.

import networkx as nx
from qiskit import QuantumCircuit

def colored_qaoa(G, gamma, beta):
    L = nx.line_graph(G)
    color = nx.coloring.greedy_color(L, strategy="largest_first")
    layers = {}
    for e, c in color.items():
        layers.setdefault(c, []).append(e)
    qc = QuantumCircuit(G.number_of_nodes())
    qc.h(range(qc.num_qubits))
    for c in sorted(layers):
        for u, v in layers[c]:
            qc.rzz(gamma, u, v)          # one matching per layer
    qc.rx(2 * beta, range(qc.num_qubits))
    return qc

What I did not verify

  • No Qiskit transpile. The SWAP count and depth after transpiling to a real or fake Heron backend are unmeasured. The lattice here is a clean model of heavy-hex, not IBM’s exact 127- or 156-qubit layout.
  • No sampling at 64 to 156 qubits. Only expected cuts were computed. A matrix-product-state simulator or hardware is needed for best-of-shots figures.
  • No noise. Everything is ideal. On a real chip, α will be lower.
  • Conventions. γ and β follow exp(−iγH_C) with H_C = ½ΣZZ and RX(2β). Qiskit’s QAOAAnsatz orders its parameters as β first, then γ, so re-optimise or map the angles before reusing them there.

Other approaches worth trying

  • CVaR-QAOA optimises the average of the best fraction of shots, which matches a “best sampled cut” score.
  • Deeper circuits (p = 2, 3) with INTERP or FOURIER initialisation lift the average cut, at the price of more noise on real chips.
  • Warm-start and recursive QAOA seed the circuit with a classical solution.
  • Mitigation: dynamical decoupling, Pauli twirling, TREX readout correction and zero-noise extrapolation in Qiskit Runtime.
